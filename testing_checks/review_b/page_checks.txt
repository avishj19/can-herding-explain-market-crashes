[markdown] The proposal's first step: the herding rule inside the model should behave exactly as the formula it comes from says.
[markdown] The herding rule inside the model comes from Kirman's ant model. 100 ants choose between two food sources; each step one ant either switches on its own (chance ε) or copies another ant. Kirman worked out the exact long-run answer.
[markdown] Below ε = 0.0091 the ants herd: most sit at one source and switch together. Now ε = 0.002, so they herd.
[markdown] **Rule:** over 20 million steps, the share of time the simulation spends at each count of ants is within 0.05 of the exact answer (0 = perfect match). Gap **0.0140**: the check <span class='ok'>passes</span>.
[markdown] The distance is the *total variation distance*: add up the gaps between the simulated and the exact share of time at every count (0 to 100 ants) and halve the sum. It is 0 for identical shares and 1 for shares that never overlap.
[markdown] With only value traders, the simulated market's daily moves should follow a bell curve, like its news.
[markdown] **Rule:** every simulated history's excess kurtosis (0 for a bell curve) is within ±0.176 of 0.
[markdown] Even a perfect bell curve gives an excess kurtosis a little away from 0 in a finite sample. The usual size of that wobble (its standard error) is √(24/n) for n days. The rule allows 5 standard errors: for n = 19,329 days, ±5·√(24/n) = ±0.176.
[markdown] Median **+0.009**; farthest from 0: **+0.063**. The check <span class='ok'>passes</span>.
[markdown] When trend chasers copy each other, big days should become about as common as in the S&P 500.
[markdown] **Rule (the proposal's):** the median tail exponent (lower = more big days) lies between 2.6 and 3.0. The inverse cubic law (Gopikrishnan et al. 1998) says about 3.
[markdown] Each history's tail exponent is the Hill estimate over its largest 5% of days (by size, up or down). The rule uses the median over the simulated histories that did not run away. **Close** means the median misses the proposal's range but sits inside the S&P 500's own uncertainty: the range its tail exponent takes over 500 resamples of its history (in runs of 50 days, so bursts stay together), middle 95%.
[markdown] Model median **3.10** (range 2.64 to 3.96) over all 40 simulated histories that did not run away; S&P 500 **2.92**. The check is <b>close</b>: the median is just above the proposal's 2.6 to 3.0; the S&P 500's own range is 2.57 to 3.32.
[markdown] The proposal's question: a bell curve says a 20-sd fall comes about once in 10⁸⁶ years, and Black Monday (23 sd) even more rarely: once in 5.0 × 10¹¹⁴ years. With herding, do falls like these come within a few decades, as in the S&P 500?
[markdown] **Rule, in two parts:** (a) falls of 8 sd, which a bell curve puts 6.4 × 10¹² years apart, come in the model at least once every 50 years; (b) so does a fall as big as Black Monday.
[markdown] (a) The model has an 8-sd fall every **35 years** (S&P 500: every 11 years): <span class='ok'>passes</span>.
[markdown] (b) Black Monday was a 23-sd fall. Bell curve: once every **5.0 × 10¹¹⁴ years**. Model: every **1,534 years** (2 such falls in 3,068 years of simulated market; very uncertain: from only 2 such falls, the true wait could be anywhere from 425 years to 12,667 years). Even the shortest of those waits is far more than 50 years: <span class='no'>does not pass</span>.
[markdown] **Overall: partly.** Herding brings the wait for a Black Monday down by about 112 powers of ten, but not all the way to the S&P 500's one in 77 years.
[markdown] A crash is a daily fall of at least that many sd (each history measured in its own sd). Years between crashes = trading days ÷ 252 ÷ number of crashes, with all held-out histories pooled. For the bell curve it is 1 ÷ (chance of such a fall on one day × 252).
[markdown] The model's big days and bursts of wild days should be about as common and as strong as the S&P 500's.
[markdown] The proposal asks for numbers comparable to the S&P 500, so there is no pass or fail line here. The model column is the median over the same histories, with the middle 90% of them in brackets (unlike check 2, whose range covers all of them).
[markdown] - **Tail exponent:** A score for how common big days are (lower = more big days). The S&P 500 is near 3: a move twice as big is about 2³ = 8 times rarer. A bell curve, measured the same way, comes out near 6.
- **Excess kurtosis:** Excess kurtosis. 0 for a bell curve; bigger means more very calm and very wild days (fat tails).
- **Memory of wild days (20 days):** Correlation of move sizes a month (20 trading days) apart. 0 means no memory; above 0 means bursts of wild days.
[markdown] Beyond the proposal: economists keep a checklist of patterns found in nearly every market (Cont, 2001). How many does herding reproduce?
[markdown] And a fair rival: **GARCH**, a formula with no traders, fitted to the same S&P 500. It also gets only bell-curve news, but its bursts are written into the formula: a big move makes the next day's typical move bigger.
[markdown] Out of 8, the model passes **5** (in most of its 40 histories), the formula **6**, and the S&P 500 **8**.
[markdown] **What that means:** herding does not beat the formula on these numbers. What it adds is a reason: the bursts come out of traders copying each other, not out of an equation chosen to have bursts. Both miss the same two patterns, which point to what the model still lacks.
[markdown] Each rule's line is set where a plain bell curve cannot reach it. The formula is GJR-GARCH(1,1) (Bollerslev 1986; Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle 1993): tomorrow's variance = 1.4e-06 + (0.032 + 0.105 after a fall) × today's squared move + 0.899 × today's variance, fitted by maximum likelihood, then run on the same 40 seeds. A count like 37/40 is how many histories pass.
[markdown] <p class='lead'>In <i>Nature</i> (vol. 397, pages 498-500), Thomas Lux and Michele Marchesi built a market of traders who switch between two strategies: <b>value traders</b> (economists call them fundamentalists), who trade on the true value, and <b>trend chasers</b> (economists call them chartists), who follow the trend and copy each other. The news in their model is plain bell-curve noise. Yet the prices came out with fat tails and bursts of wild days. Their claim: those come from the traders, not from the news. This project rebuilt their model from its equations and tested that claim in two ways.</p>
[markdown] This project fits the model's settings in two ways. **Paper-matched** settings are fitted to the numbers the paper reports; **S&P-calibrated** settings are fitted to the S&P 500. Section 1 uses the first, section 2 the second.

[markdown] Ours: the mean of 8 simulated histories of 100,000 days each, on seeds never used to fit the settings (they were used to choose between fits), ± how much they vary from one history to the next. The tail exponent here is measured the paper's way: a straight line fitted to the largest 30% of days. The *Nature* letter gives no settings (the follow-up paper with its tables was not yet obtained), so ours are fitted to its numbers. The match holds with the model's time step of 0.01 day, as in the paper. With a finer time step these settings lose most of their fat tails. Like the paper, the fat tails fade over longer spans: excess kurtosis (0 for a bell curve) falls from 43 for one day to 0.4 for 25 days. **Not reproduced:** Hurst H of move size: 0.81 against 0.85. Our bursts of wild days are a little weaker than the paper's.
[markdown] Hurst H is a memory score where 0.5 means no memory. It is a different score from the *memory of wild days* on the other pages, where 0 means no memory.
[markdown] Settings fitted to the S&P 500, 40 simulated histories as long as its record. Here the tail exponent is the Hill estimate over the largest 5% of days, as on the rest of the site:
- **Value traders only:** a bell curve (median excess kurtosis 0.01, where a bell curve gives 0). Bell-curve news alone gives bell-curve moves.
- **Trend chasers, no herding:** the price runs away in 40 of 40 simulated histories.
- **Trend chasers who herd:** fat tails with a median tail exponent of **3.10**, against **2.92** for the S&P 500 and about 3 for markets in general (lower = more big days).
[markdown] By the paper's own method (largest 30% of days), the S&P 500's tail exponent is 2.35: fatter than every one of the model's simulated histories in the project's [report](https://github.com/avishj19/can-herding-explain-market-crashes/blob/main/figures/RESULTS.md#fig-7--is-the-sp-500-a-typical-history-of-the-model) (Fig 7). Its middle-to-large days are more common than the model's.
[markdown] <p class='lead'><b>Our finding:</b> the paper's main claim holds in our rebuild. Herding alone, with plain bell-curve news, gives the fat tails and bursts of wild days of a real market. It does not explain everything. The S&P 500 falls harder than it rises, and the model does not. The model's prices reverse a little from one day to the next, and the S&P 500's do not. The model loses its fat tails over a few weeks somewhat faster than the S&P 500. Its settings are fitted, not the paper's own, and they rarely run away into an endless bubble. <a href='https://github.com/avishj19/can-herding-explain-market-crashes/blob/main/docs/KEY_POINTS.md#7-what-the-model-gets-wrong-honest-limitations'>All the limitations</a> (KEY_POINTS, section 7).</p>
